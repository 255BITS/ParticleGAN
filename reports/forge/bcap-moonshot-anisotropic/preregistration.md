The hypothesis is that isotropic local smoothing confuses narrow and wide directions, so real-neighborhood covariance provides a better local density force. For each real-derived anchor a, covariance C comes from its sixteen nearest other reference rows. Define H=C+max(.1 trace(C)/d,epsilon times global real coordinate variance) I and phi_m(z)=exp(-(z-a)^T H^-1(z-a)/(2m²)), m∈{1,2,4}. The auxiliary objective is mean_(a,m)[((mean_fake phi_m-mean_real phi_m)/mean_real phi_m)²]. All geometry and normalizers are detached. Each anchor appears in the complete real mean, so its denominator is at least1/n. Fake gradients reach the existing generated branch and learned prior; original conditional identity objectives remain responsible for pairing.

The force is derived by differentiating the frozen feature frame: for each fake row z, gradient L is the sum of -2(q-p) phi_m(z) H^-1(z-a)/(n m² p²) over anchor/scale coordinates, divided by their count. A local deficit attracts nearby outputs and a surplus repels them in the real covariance metric. This is a finite empirical feature MMD interpretation, with a positive-semidefinite kernel defined by the feature dot product. Real-derived, batch-dependent finite features supply neither a calibrated hypothesis test nor a characteristic population divergence.

[Gretton et al.](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v13/gretton12a.html) supply the mean-feature discrepancy interpretation. [Olsen et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/olsen24a.html) use nearest-neighborhood covariance to inform adaptive density kernels. This candidate borrows that geometry principle; it implements no Bayesian density inference or guarantee from those papers.
